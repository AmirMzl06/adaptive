I wanted to provide a comprehensive update on the d′-PGD experiments following our previous discussion and the modifications to the Acorn-dprime fork.

After the fork revision, I completed two experimental batches:

1. **Exp3:** 12 configurations × 3 seeds = 36 training runs.
2. **Matched Madry isotropic controls:** 2 configurations × 3 seeds = 6 additional runs.

All **42 post-revision runs completed successfully** using four H200 GPUs, with results and diagnostics saved independently for each seed.

The experiments were designed to address a specific question: can we improve the existing PGD-based CEBRA training approach by introducing a more principled, noise-informed perturbation geometry, even when epsilon remains a fixed hyperparameter?

A second objective—automatic epsilon selection—remains relevant, but was not directly tested in these final 42 runs.

Below are the complete results and my interpretation.

---

## 1. Experimental motivation and fork revision

Our earlier experiments suggested that some d′-based configurations improved downstream decoder R², but interpretation was complicated by the automatic epsilon calibration and potential differences in stochastic sampling.

In the previous four-model diagnostic ablation (seed 42), we obtained:

| Configuration | Validation R² |
|---|---:|
| MADRY, fixed ε=1 | 0.536874 |
| MADRY, automatic instance ε | 0.543218 |
| TRADES, fixed ε=1 | 0.600654 |
| TRADES, automatic instance ε | 0.585947 |

Although these results were promising for TRADES, the automatically calibrated epsilon remained at approximately 1 and the instance-specific nearest-neighbor constraint was never activated.

Thus, these experiments did not establish that the automatic procedure was selecting meaningfully different perturbation budgets.

Following these observations, the fork was revised to address deterministic sampling, isolate calibration randomness, correct the zero-curriculum epsilon behavior, improve epsilon-distribution logging, provide additional covariance-approximation diagnostics, and support isotropic control experiments.

The subsequent 42 runs used the revised fork.

## 2. Experimental setup

**Neural data**

- Perich session: C-CO12
- Number of neurons: 86
- Input window: 36 time bins
- Three independent training seeds: 42, 43, 44

**CEBRA encoder**

- Architecture: `offset36-model-more-dropout`
- Time-only contrastive training
- Latent dimension: 64
- Hidden dimension: 64
- Temperature: 0.4
- Batch size: 2,048
- Training iterations: 3,000

**Downstream evaluation**

- Two-layer MLP decoder
- Hidden dimension: 64
- Decoder dropout: 0.4
- Training epochs: 2,500
- Primary metric: clean validation decoder R²

CEBRA was trained on neural activity without behavioral supervision. Behavioral labels were introduced only when training and evaluating the downstream decoder.

Importantly, the same dropout-containing CEBRA architecture was used for every experimental condition. No configuration was given a different encoder architecture or additional dropout.

**Adversarial training**

The conventional reference used raw-input L2-PGD with ε=3.

The proposed d′ configurations used:

- Stationary noise modeling
- Whole-recording residuals
- APGD optimization
- 10 attack steps
- One restart
- Zero warmup and zero curriculum
- No physiological guards
- Fixed epsilon values
- No instance-adaptive epsilon selection

The noise-aware perturbation model used Ledoit–Wolf spatial covariance and Welch temporal spectral modeling.

The isotropic controls used isotropic spatial covariance and white temporal structure.

For TRADES, the coefficient was β=6. The Madry configurations did not use the TRADES KL-consistency objective.

All reported R² values are measured on clean validation inputs.

### 3.1. Epsilon dependence

For Madry, performance increased monotonically across the tested budgets:

| Epsilon | Mean R² |
|---|---:|
| Approximately 0 | 0.431122 |
| 1 | 0.538739 |
| 3 | 0.602336 |
| 8 | 0.617484 |

TRADES exhibited a different pattern:

| Epsilon | Mean R² |
|---|---:|
| Approximately 0 | 0.483704 |
| 1 | 0.587138 |
| 3 | 0.591650 |
| 8 | 0.505839 |

The high-budget TRADES model was particularly unstable, with seed 44 achieving only 0.444293.

This indicates that perturbation strength interacts strongly with the adversarial training objective.

### 3.2. Dropout-consistency hypothesis

Since the encoder contains dropout and TRADES uses a KL consistency term, we included near-zero epsilon controls to assess whether the gains could be explained primarily by stochastic consistency regularization.

However, TRADES D1 consistently outperformed TRADES D0:

| Seed | TRADES D0 | TRADES D1 | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 42 | 0.517450 | 0.590143 | +0.072692 |
| 43 | 0.456794 | 0.591219 | +0.134425 |
| 44 | 0.476868 | 0.580051 | +0.103182 |

The average improvement was +0.103433 R².

Furthermore, the best noise-aware Madry configurations achieved substantial improvements without the TRADES KL objective.

Therefore, the data do not support explaining all gains solely as a by-product of TRADES-induced dropout consistency.

However, an explicit dropout-free architectural ablation has not been performed, so the independent contribution of ordinary encoder dropout remains unresolved.

### 3.3. Initial geometry comparison within TRADES

Exp3 included isotropic controls at ε=1 and ε=3.

At ε=1:

- Noise-aware TRADES: 0.587138
- Isotropic TRADES: 0.563378
- Difference: +0.023760 in favor of noise-aware geometry.

At ε=3:

- Noise-aware TRADES: 0.591650
- Isotropic TRADES: 0.612095
- Difference: −0.020446 for noise-aware geometry.

Both directions were consistent across all three seeds.

This motivated a more direct geometry comparison under the Madry objective, which had produced our strongest noise-aware result.

## 5. Final experiment: matched Madry isotropic controls

To more directly isolate perturbation geometry, I performed a second experimental batch using exactly the Madry objective rather than comparing Madry against isotropic TRADES.

The additional configurations were:

- MADRY_ISO_D3
- MADRY_ISO_D8

All other intended training settings were kept consistent with the corresponding noise-aware Madry configurations, including APGD, 10 attack steps, one restart, zero curriculum, no guards, the same CEBRA architecture, and the same three seeds.

Only the spatial and temporal noise-model settings were changed together:

- Noise-aware: Ledoit–Wolf + Welch.
- Isotropic: Isotropic + White.

All six new runs completed successfully.

## 8. Interpretation

I think the combined results support four findings.

**1. The modified PGD-based training configurations can improve downstream decoding substantially.**

Across the three seeds on C-CO12, several configurations exceeded the conventional L2-PGD baseline.

The strongest overall improvement was achieved by isotropic Madry d′-APGD at ε=8.

**2. The choice of perturbation budget matters considerably.**

Madry improved across the tested fixed budgets, while TRADES degraded at the highest budget and showed greater seed variability.

This suggests that the training objective and perturbation magnitude should be considered jointly.

**3. Covariance-aware geometry has a regime-dependent benefit rather than a universal advantage.**

The matched-objective experiments provide the clearest evidence:

- At ε=3, the noise-aware geometry outperformed isotropic geometry in all three seeds, with a mean advantage of +0.036012 R².
- At ε=8, isotropic geometry achieved the higher mean R² by +0.010752.

The TRADES comparisons showed a similar qualitative interaction, with noise-aware geometry performing better at ε=1 and isotropic geometry performing better at ε=3.

However, because we change both spatial and temporal covariance modeling and do not yet match raw-input perturbation magnitudes, the exact source of these effects remains uncertain.

**4. The automatic epsilon objective remains unresolved.**

The final 42 runs investigated fixed perturbation budgets.

Earlier experiments did not demonstrate meaningful instance-dependent epsilon selection, and the automatic calibration chose approximately ε=1 despite better fixed-budget performance at larger values in subsequent experiments.

Therefore, the current evidence supports further work on intelligent perturbation geometry more strongly than it supports a claim of successful automatic epsilon selection.

## 9. Limitations and implications for a potential methodological contribution

I think we should distinguish the experimentally supported result from the stronger methodological claim we would eventually like to make.

We have evidence that changing the PGD-based adversarial training configuration improves clean validation R².

We do not yet have sufficient evidence that the improvement is specifically caused by neural noise covariance, because:

1. Equal numerical d′ budgets do not guarantee equal raw-input L2 perturbation magnitudes.
2. The geometry control changes both spatial covariance and temporal whitening together.
3. The original PGD baseline also differs in attack optimization and budget geometry.
4. We have not performed independent held-out adversarial robustness evaluations.
5. The multi-seed comparisons are currently limited to one recording session.
6. The best configuration was identified from a set of tested alternatives, so its apparent advantage should be confirmed independently.

Additionally, ε values of 3 and 8 exceed the nominal d′=1 reference used in the original ideal-observer calibration framework. Better decoder performance at these budgets does not establish that the corresponding perturbations remain physiologically subtle.

The high numerical robustness margins of the isotropic models also require caution: margins expressed in different covariance metrics cannot be interpreted as directly comparable physical perturbation strengths.

## 10. Proposed next step

My preferred next experiment would be a matched-effective-budget comparison.

Specifically, we could measure the raw-input L2 magnitude of the actual perturbations generated by noise-aware and isotropic APGD, then adjust the budgets so the methods produce comparable perturbation magnitudes while keeping the Madry objective and all other training settings fixed.

This would help distinguish a genuine benefit of covariance-informed attack geometry from an effect caused primarily by perturbation scale.

A subsequent factorized comparison of spatial covariance and temporal spectral modeling would also clarify which component contributes most.

If the noise-aware advantage persists under these controls, we would have stronger evidence for a neural-data-specific Smart PGD contribution.

If it does not, the results would still reveal useful improvements from the optimization and budget choices, but the central methodological claim would need to be adjusted.

I would be very interested in your thoughts on the reversal between noise-aware and isotropic geometry as epsilon increases, and whether you think the best next direction is to isolate the covariance contribution further or revisit the automatic epsilon-selection mechanism.
