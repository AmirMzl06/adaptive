I ran two sets of experiments using the **Acorn-dprime** fork. The first experiment evaluated 24 adversarial-training configurations covering the main implemented ideas. Based on those results, I ran a second experiment with four controlled configurations to investigate whether the observed improvements came primarily from TRADES or from instance-adaptive epsilon.

Below are the experimental results, calibration diagnostics, and observations from reviewing the implementation.

# 2. Experiment 1 — Comparison of 24 Configurations

The first experiment compared standard PGD against the different components implemented in Acorn-dprime, including:

- Stationary d′-PGD and APGD
- Automatic and instance-adaptive epsilon calibration
- Alternative spatial covariance estimators
- Alternative temporal covariance models
- Nonstationary trial-level masking
- TRADES adversarial training
- Physiological guards and their combinations

The original PGD baselines used ε=3 with L2 and L∞ norms. The d′ attacks used whitened L2 geometry, so their numerical epsilon values are not directly equivalent to the original input-space PGD budgets.

The default d′ configurations used Ledoit–Wolf spatial covariance, Welch temporal modeling, APGD, and one restart. Warmup and curriculum fractions were initially 0.10 and 0.50, except where explicitly overridden.

## Main Observations

The stationary d′ methods performed slightly better than standard L2-PGD under the tested settings.

For example:

- Standard L2-PGD: R² = 0.563620
- Stationary d′-PGD Fixed3: R² = 0.568449
- Stationary d′-APGD Fixed3: R² = 0.574148

The strongest result came from **TRADES Auto Instance, with R² = 0.581986**.

However, most other automatically calibrated configurations performed worse than the fixed-epsilon d′ configurations.

The nonstationary trial-mask variants and the physiological guards also did not improve clean validation R² in these experiments.

In particular, the band-power and aperiodic guards reduced the realized mean d′ to approximately 0.29, indicating that the effective perturbations were substantially restricted.

These observations motivated a second experiment to isolate the effects of the training objective and epsilon adaptation.

# 3. Experiment 2 — Four-Model Controlled Ablation

To investigate why TRADES Auto Instance performed well, I compared four configurations:

1. Madry + Fixed ε=1
2. Madry + Auto Instance ε
3. TRADES + Fixed ε=1
4. TRADES + Auto Instance ε

All four configurations used:

- Stationary d′ geometry
- APGD with one restart
- Ledoit–Wolf spatial covariance
- Welch temporal covariance
- 10 attack steps
- No physiological guards
- Warmup = 0
- Curriculum fraction = 0
- TRADES β = 6.0, where applicable
- Identical CEBRA and decoder settings
- Seed = 42

Although the curriculum fraction was set to zero, the implementation still uses one curriculum step. Consequently, epsilon starts at 0.25 for the first step and reaches its final value of 1.0 afterward.

## Performance Differences

The second experiment produced an even stronger result for TRADES.

**TRADES Fixed1 achieved R² = 0.600654, the highest value observed across both experimental suites.**

The differences were:

| Comparison | Absolute R² Difference |
|---|---:|
| TRADES Fixed1 − Madry Fixed1 | +0.063780 |

Compared with the original L2-PGD baseline (R² = 0.563620), TRADES Fixed1 achieved an improvement of approximately **+0.037034 R²**.

This suggests that the TRADES objective may be particularly beneficial for learning useful CEBRA representations in this setting.

However, the result is currently based on one session and one seed. Additionally, Madry uses separate clean and adversarial optimizer updates in the current implementation, whereas TRADES uses one update on a combined objective. This difference should also be considered when interpreting the results.

# 4. Adaptive Epsilon Calibration Analysis

A particularly important observation emerged from the epsilon calibration reports.

The intended instance-adaptive budget is:

\[
\epsilon_i=\min(\epsilon^*,\epsilon_{NN,i})
\]

where:

\[
\epsilon^*=\min(\epsilon_{JND},\epsilon_{NN},\epsilon_{det})
\]

The automatic calibration produced:

| Calibration Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| ε_JND | 1.0 |
| ε_NN (10th percentile) | 71.3416 |
| Median nearest-neighbor distance | 73.9924 |
| ε_det | 1.0 |
| Final ε* | 1.0 |
| NN binding rate | 0.0 |
| Nearest-neighbor bank size | 2,000 |
| Number of NN queries | 18,349 |

The reports also showed:

```text
label_kind = null
classes = null
```

This confirms that behavioral labels were not used for the nearest-neighbor calibration in these runs.

The important observation is that nearest-neighbor distances are approximately 71, while the final epsilon budget is capped at 1.

As a result, none of the training references had their epsilon reduced by the nearest-neighbor constraint.

Therefore, **the instance-adaptive mechanism was enabled, but it did not produce different effective per-reference budgets in these experiments**.

This is a significant finding because the Fixed and Auto configurations were effectively trained with the same epsilon schedule.

The detector criterion was AUC ≤ 0.55.

At ε=1, the detectors remained below this threshold. At ε=2, the threshold was exceeded.

Therefore, the calibrated detector budget was:

\[
\epsilon_{det}=1
\]

Combined with ε_JND=1 and ε_NN≈71, this resulted in:

\[
\epsilon^*=1
\]

These detector measurements support the choice of ε=1 under the current calibration protocol, but do not independently establish physiological indistinguishability.

One observation worth investigating is the relatively high Kronecker approximation error.

The current stationary d′ model assumes a separable spatial–temporal covariance structure:

\[
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mathrm{spatial}}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathrm{temporal}}
\]

However, the reported relative error of approximately 0.75 suggests that this approximation may not capture all dependencies in the neural recordings.

This does not necessarily invalidate the stationary model, but it would be useful to evaluate how much its approximation quality affects the attack geometry.

# 8. Possible RNG Issue in the Implementation

After inspecting `cebra/dprime/training.py` and `calibration.py`, I noticed a possible source of inconsistency between Fixed and Auto experiments.

The trainer initializes a dedicated random generator:

```python
self.generator = torch.Generator().manual_seed(
    int(solver.adv_dprime_seed)
)
```

However, the Auto configuration calls `_calibrate()` before adversarial training.

During calibration, the same generator is passed into the detector-calibration attacks:

```python
adv, info = attacks.dprime_pgd(
    ...,
    generator=self.generator
)
```

The generator is also consumed by the colored-noise and L∞ attack paths used during calibration.

The Fixed configuration does not execute this calibration procedure.

Therefore, even when the initial seed and effective epsilon budgets are identical, the two configurations can enter adversarial training with **different random generator states**.

As a result, the APGD random starts and subsequent stochastic trajectories may differ.

This is one plausible explanation for the observed R² differences between Fixed and Auto despite their effectively identical epsilon budgets.

The calibration procedure may also consume global CPU/CUDA RNG states through neural-network initialization and detector training.

This hypothesis requires a controlled experiment before attributing the performance differences to RNG behavior.

# 9. Suggested Code Modification

A useful first modification would be to separate the calibration RNG from the adversarial-training RNG.

For example, the current training code:

```python
if self.eps_star is None and not warm:
    self._calibrate(batch)
```

could be modified to preserve the dedicated attack generator state:

```python
if self.eps_star is None and not warm:
    attack_rng_state = self.generator.get_state().clone()

    try:
        self._calibrate(batch)
    finally:
        self.generator.set_state(attack_rng_state)
```

This would prevent detector calibration from advancing the dedicated attack generator used later in training.

For a stricter comparison, it would also be useful to isolate the global PyTorch CPU/CUDA RNG states consumed during calibration, potentially using `torch.random.fork_rng`.

After this modification, Fixed1 and Auto Instance could be rerun with matched random streams to determine whether their performance differences persist.

An additional useful improvement would be to log the actual distribution of effective epsilon values across training references:

- Minimum ε
- 10th percentile
- Median
- 90th percentile
- Maximum ε
- Fraction of samples constrained by nearest-neighbor distance

This would directly verify whether the instance-adaptive mechanism is functioning as intended.

# 10. Overall Interpretation

The results suggest several conclusions.

**1. Stationary d′-based adversarial training is promising.**

The stationary d′-PGD/APGD configurations slightly outperformed standard L2-PGD under the tested settings, although their epsilon scales are not directly equivalent.

**2. TRADES appears to be the most promising component so far.**

The best observed result was:

\[
\boxed{\text{TRADES Fixed1: }R^2=0.600654}
\]

Both TRADES configurations substantially outperformed their corresponding Madry configurations in the second experiment.

**3. Instance-adaptive epsilon has not yet demonstrated an independent benefit.**

The nearest-neighbor constraint never became active, so the automatic configurations effectively used the same epsilon schedule as the fixed configurations.

**4. The Fixed-versus-Auto comparison may be influenced by RNG-state differences.**

The detector calibration consumes randomness before training, and this may cause two nominally comparable configurations to follow different optimization trajectories.

**5. The covariance approximation and spike-count-specific noise model deserve further validation.**

The Kronecker approximation error is relatively high, and the covariance estimated from the whole recording may contain behavior-related neural variability as well as stochastic noise.

## Suggested Next Steps

I would prioritize the following:

1. Isolate calibration and training RNG streams, then repeat the four-model ablation under controlled randomness.
2. Verify and log the actual per-reference epsilon distribution.
3. Investigate a genuinely adaptive, statistically calibrated epsilon mechanism for binned spike counts, without arbitrarily increasing the JND threshold.
4. Repeat the strongest configurations across multiple seeds and recording sessions.
5. Evaluate adversarial robustness separately from clean validation R².
6. Examine whether improved residual-based noise estimation or alternative covariance structures provide a better fit to the spike-count data.

Overall, **the strongest experimental signal so far is the combination of stationary d′-APGD and TRADES**, rather than the current instance-adaptive epsilon mechanism.

The result of R²=0.600654 is encouraging, but the next important step is to separate the benefit of the TRADES objective from the effects of epsilon calibration, random-number consumption, and the training procedure.

These results also identify a clear opportunity to improve the adaptive-epsilon component while preserving the main noise-aware geometry of the proposed attack.
